# Arablyzer — M3.1: the pre-launch security review

> What the pre-launch security review found (2026-09-30), by the part of the stack it concerns: what was
> found, what changed, what was measured, and what is left. Numbers are from 2026-09-30, measured in the
> scanner image (Node 24.21, and the container limits Compose gives the scanner: 1200 MB, a V8 heap of 696 MB)
> unless a row says "host": Node 22.22 on Apple silicon. Written as the work goes.

---

## Scanner

Two findings, both in what runs a scan: **H1** (GitHub issue #28) and **M3** (#31).

### H1: one page ended the scanner, and every scan queued behind it

**Found.** A page of 1.4 million `<p>` is 4 KB gzipped and 4.2 MB read, under every limit a scan had (25 MB
fetched, 15 MB parsed). Its tree took more heap than Node has in the scanner's container, and Node ended the
whole process: reproduced in the scanner image, exit status 134, "Ineffective mark-compacts near heap limit".
The worker's next requests were refused, and "the scanner is not there" was final: a scan is tried once, so
every scan queued behind the one that died failed within moments, until Compose had started the scanner again.

**Changed**, in three layers, so that no one guess has to be right about every page:

1. **Limits on the tree, in the tree adapter** (`packages/collectors/src/page.ts`). At most **200,000 nodes**
   (elements and comments: a comment is 7 bytes of page and 200 of heap) and **10,000 levels** of nesting; the
   parse ends at the first element past either, and the page is `page-too-complex`, as it is when parsing runs
   out of time. Neither reads the clock, so the same page is too complex on every machine. The nesting limit
   is what makes 100,000 nested `<div>` (0.5 MB) end at once: parse5 works through the open elements with
   many start tags, so nesting costs time quadratic in its depth, and the deadline that ended it did so after
   the full 30 s (measured on the host: 5,000 levels 0.10 s, 20,000 1.3 s, 50,000 8.3 s, 100,000 31 s).
   Chromium's own parser stops at 512 levels (`kMaximumHTMLParserDOMTreeDepth`); 10,000 leaves room for pages
   that are broken, and costs 0.3 s at most. The notice's copy no longer says "within the time limit"
   (Arabic and English; the Arabic needs the owner's reading).
2. **The HTML is read in a thread with a heap and a clock of its own** (`collectPageIsolated`, the scanner's
   `isolateParse`). The limits above cannot say which mix of nodes and bytes fits a heap: 199,994 links of
   72 bytes are 14.4 MB, pass every limit, and need 600 MB, nearly all the 696 MB that Node has in the
   container (host, the smallest heap that survives, in steps of 50 MB; before the limits, 150 MB for 200,000
   `<p>`, 250 with Arabic text, 450 for 200,000 short links, 400 for 2 million comments). And nothing in the tree adapter can stop a parse that never reaches it: one start tag with many attributes
   is quadratic in parse5's tokenizer (20,000 attributes 0.6 s, 40,000 2.9 s, 80,000 15.9 s; 15 MB of them
   would take hours), and it held the scanner's event loop, its health answer and its own hard limit the whole
   time. The thread has `resourceLimits.maxOldGenerationSizeMb` of 384; a timer ends it wherever it is
   (`terminate()`); running out of either is `page-too-complex`; any other failure is `page-unreadable`. It runs
   the same `readHtml` as the collector, so the facts are the same (tests compare them over a rich page, two
   encodings, XHTML, a truncated page, and the reports of every twentieth rule fixture). The CLI still reads in
   its own process (its bundle stays one file, and the limits above apply to it too); the scanner turns the
   thread on in `scanOptionsFrom`. The thread needs no flag of its process: it registers `tsx` itself
   (`--import tsx` did not reach a thread on Node 22.22), and `collectors` now declares `tsx`.
3. **The worker waits for a scanner that is not there** (`apps/worker/src/run.ts`, `ScannerUnavailable` in
   `packages/scanner-client`). A refused connection, a name not found, an address unreachable, a connection
   that timed out, or a 503 before the scanner sent a line: the scan has not begun, so the worker asks again
   after 0.5 s, then 1, 2, 4 and 5 s at a time, for a minute in all. Measured against Docker's restart policy,
   a container being started refuses the connection, and one waiting for a long restart has no name in Docker's
   DNS: both are "not there". Not a reset, a closed socket, a timeout after the connection, or any other status:
   those may come after the scan began, and a scan is still never run twice behind the visitor's back
   (BullMQ's `attempts` stay one). A scanner that is back within the minute
   fails nothing, and one that is not fails that scan alone.

**Measured** (scanner image, unless "host")

| Page | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| 1.4 million `<p>` | the process aborts (exit 134) | `page-too-complex` in 0.4 s; without the limit on nodes, the thread runs out of its heap and this process lives on (9.5 s) |
| 100,000 nested `<div>` | 31 s to the deadline (host) | `page-too-complex` in 0.6 s |
| 2 million comments (14 MB) | 393 MB of heap (host) | `page-too-complex` in 0.25 s |
| one tag with 300,000 attributes | minutes, and no answer to anything | the thread ends at its timer (2.0 s asked, 2.006 s taken) and this thread's event loop ticks meanwhile |
| 130,000 links (12 MB) | 384 to 512 MB of heap (host) | `page-too-complex` at the thread's 384 MB, in 4.9 s |
| 60,000 links (5.6 MB) | 1.4 s, process at 408 MB | read whole in 1.4 s, process at 474 MB after |
| a page at the limits, whole scan, all rules, no browser (host) | | 0.4 s at 10,000 levels; 1.3 s at 200,000 paragraphs; 1.7 s at 199,994 links; 1.1 s at 39,000 table rows; each 0.3 to 0.7 s more in the thread |
| a tiny page | 14 ms | 131 ms: the thread starts, registers `tsx` and loads the collector |

The worker, against a real scanner container whose Node process was killed (`SIGKILL`) in the middle of a
scan, four scans in a row. Before: the scan in flight failed with no report (it had begun: "terminated"), and
the three behind it failed 7, 2 and 2 ms later, "The scanner is not there (ECONNREFUSED)", with no report.
After: the scan in flight failed the same way, and the three behind it ran, each with its report: the first
after 1.6 s, for the scanner Compose started again (two refused asks), the other two at once.

**Left.**

- The limits are measured on pages made for the purpose. No real page was measured: pages with more than a few
  thousand elements are rare, and the notice will say when one meets a limit. Count `page-too-complex` once the
  site is public, and move a limit only with a page in hand.
- A scan that was in flight when its scanner died fails: it had begun. A scanner that stays away for more than
  a minute fails each scan that waits for it, one minute at a time.
- The CLI reads in its own process, so a start tag with hundreds of thousands of attributes still holds a CLI
  scan for minutes: the machine of whoever runs it, and no one else's.
- The thread's facts are copied back into this process (structured clone): for a moment a big page's facts
  exist twice. The heap is 384 MB so that this process, the thread and the copy fit the container's 1200 MB
  with room; the browsers start after the thread has ended.

### M3: a browser that a page took over could outlive its scan

**Found.** The browsers run without a sandbox of their own (Playwright's default, `chromiumSandbox: false`; the
lab passes `--no-sandbox`; the container is the boundary, M1.3c), as the scanner's own user, in the process
namespace of the Node process that holds the worker's token and the CrUX key, and that process lived for as
many scans as came. A renderer that a page took over could read that process's environment and memory and go
on to alter the reports of the scans after its own: those reports are stored as the visitors'.

**Changed.**

- **The scanner serves one scan and ends its process** (`apps/scanner`): `createScannerApp({ onServed })`
  is called when the scan has ended, however it ended (a report, an error, a worker that hung up, a limit); the
  scanner then takes no scan, and `retireAfterAnswer` (`retire.ts`) stops it listening and ends the process when
  the scan's answer, which asks for its connection to close, has gone and the process has lived 11 s. A
  connection that will not go is waited for 5 s. Compose's `restart: unless-stopped` starts the container
  again, and a new container starts clean: measured, nothing a process writes to the tmpfs, and no process it
  leaves running, survives a restart by the policy.
- **`/health` says what is true.** `ok` while the scanner can take a scan, and while one runs within its
  limit; `stuck` once one has run past it (as before, and the scanner ends itself 30 s later); `restarting`,
  with a 503, once it has served its scan, and so does any scan asked of it (`{"error":"restarting"}`), which
  the worker reads as a scanner that is not there yet. A wrong token, a body that is not a scan, or a scan
  turned away as busy is not a scan served. Nothing ends the process where `onServed` is not given (the
  development API).
- **The worker tolerates it**: item 3 of H1 above. Its wait is a minute; a scanner that had lived long was
  taking scans again 1.6 s after the last. The same run of four scans, with the scanner ending its process
  after each, waited 1.6, 12.5 and 12.6 s (the last two are the minimum uptime, below).
- **The stack's test** waits for the scanner as the worker does before it looks inside the scanner, and has a
  test of its own: a file and a process left in the scanner are gone after a scan, and its container has been
  started again. Not run: see below.

**Why 11 s.** Docker doubles the pause before it restarts a container that ended by itself, from 100 ms to a
minute, for as long as the container lives under ten seconds (Docker 29.5.2, Colima on aarch64: the starts of
a container that lived a second were 2.6, 3.0, 1.6, 2.0, 2.9, 4.5, 7.5, 14.1 and 26.9 s apart; those of one
that lived eleven, 11.4 to 13.3 s). A scanner that served quick scans one after another would wait longer for
each. Measured, with no minimum uptime, twelve scans of about 0.1 s each: the second to the tenth waited 1.6,
1.5, 3.5, 3.6, 3.6, 7.6, 12.6, 17.5 and 27.5 s, the eleventh 52.8 s, and the twelfth found no scanner for the
minute the worker waits, and failed (of the sixty-four asks that were refused in that run, seventeen were
`ECONNREFUSED`, a container being started, and forty-seven `ENOTFOUND`, one waiting for its restart). With the
process living 11 s at the least, each waits 1.6 s once (a scanner that had lived long) and 12.5 s after that,
however quick the scan.

**Cost.** Every scan pays the restart. A scan that takes longer than 11 s pays the 1.6 s the scanner takes to
start; a quick one (the scan of a page refused at the door took 56 ms; a tool page's, which starts no browser,
takes the time of its fetches) holds the scanner for 12.5 s, about five of them a minute. That is the price of
"after every scan"; the last item below is the way to lower it.

**Left: what a takeover can still do.**

- **In its own scan.** The browsers and the Node process are still one user, in one process namespace: a
  renderer that a page took over can read the token and the CrUX key from `/proc`, and forge the report of the
  scan it took over, which is the scan of a page its author asked for. It reaches no other visitor's scan: a
  process takes one scan, and the next scan's process is a new one.
- **The secrets.** The token opens only the scanner, on the network the worker and the scanner share; a copy of
  it leaves the container only through the egress proxy, which refuses private ranges and nothing else (the
  browsers reach the internet through it). The CrUX key opens the Chrome UX Report API; restrict it there
  (`infra/.env.example`), and a stolen copy costs quota and nothing else.
- **A process that will not end.** `onStuck` and the process's own exit are Node's: a takeover that holds the
  process holds them, and Docker does not restart a container for being unhealthy. The scan's answer is the
  worker's timeout (240 s), and the container stays as it is. A supervisor that restarts unhealthy containers
  (Compose has none) would close this.
- **A container escape** is what stands between a takeover and the host, and is as it was: no capabilities, a
  read-only root, no new privileges, a cap on processes, no route but the egress proxy.
- **Quick scans are slow** (Cost). A scan that started no browser has run no page's code in the process, and
  could skip the exit; the engine would have to say so, and this branch did what the review asked, after every
  scan.

**The stronger option: browsers with nothing to steal.** Separate the browsers from the secrets, and the exit
after each scan stops being what holds everything up.

1. Run the browsers in a container of their own (`launchServer` there, `connect` from the scanner over the
   network they share): no token, no CrUX key, no engine, on the `isolated` network alone, ending its process
   after each scan as the scanner does now. A takeover then reaches a container with nothing in it but the page
   it took. It costs a second image (the browsers, without the engine), a protocol version that has to match
   the Playwright and Lighthouse in the scanner, and screenshots, fonts and files that cross a connection.
2. Or take the secrets out of the process that runs pages: the worker or the API asks CrUX (the page's URL goes
   to Google from there), and the token gives way to the network boundary the scanner already has (the worker
   is the only one on it). Then there is nothing in the scanner to read.
3. Either way, give Chromium its own sandbox back: a seccomp profile that allows the namespaces it needs, in
   place of `--no-sandbox` (M1.3c named it), so that a renderer takeover has to be followed by an escape from
   the sandbox before it reaches the process.

Recommended: 1 and 3, when the scanner has a host of its own; 2 is small and can come first.

**Not run here.** `pnpm test:stack`: its containers and its test network take the names and the subnet of the
stack the owner has running, so it waits for a Docker with none. What was run instead, in the scanner image
built from this branch and under the limits Compose gives the scanner: the hostile pages above, a scan through
the scanner's own options, and a private scanner container with a restart policy, the worker's `runScan` and
its real client against it (four scans, twelve without the minimum uptime, and one with the scanner killed in
the middle of a scan).
